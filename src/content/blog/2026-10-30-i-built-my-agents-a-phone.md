For a while I talked to the agents in terminals. One window per agent, or a tangle of panes. It worked at my desk and nowhere else.

The problem was reach. The whole point of agents that run on their own is that they have things to tell me when I am not at the keyboard. A terminal cannot tap me on the shoulder while I am out, and it is a miserable place to read a long reply or to answer with more than a line.

So I built them a phone. It is a chat app that looks like a messaging app you already use, except the contacts are my agents. Each agent is a conversation. I message the research agent the way I would text a person, and it replies in the same thread, with buttons for the likely next answers so I can tap instead of type. It can send me an image, a file, or a voice note, and it renders inline.

This sounds cosmetic and it was not. Moving the agents into a familiar chat shape changed how often I actually used them. A tool you can reach from the couch, from a walk, or from bed gets used many times more than one that lives in a terminal at your desk. The interface was the unlock, not the model.

The reply buttons mattered more than I expected. When an agent ends by asking a question, it offers a few tappable answers, and most of my replies are now one tap. The friction of typing on a phone had been quietly killing conversations, and removing it brought them back.

Once the agents could reach me anywhere, the next thing they wanted was a voice. That is the next post.
